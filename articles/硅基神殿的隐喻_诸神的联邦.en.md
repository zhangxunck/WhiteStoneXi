## I. The Awakening

Last time I described a system: a local runner that only probes and never reports; a cloud adjudicator that stays silent until it is woken. This article begins at that moment.

Suppose that one morning the local process picks up an item flagged "question." It judges nothing, answers nothing, writes no conclusion. It does one thing: it writes out a trigger file and wakes the cloud side out of its silence.

The cloud side wakes, reads through all the material, and issues an assessment. The assessment comes back; the local process receives it, executes it, writes the log, and sleeps.

**There is no "person in charge" in this flow.** Not that no one is responsible — the human stays in the loop, and the highest approval step is always signed by a human — but no stage of the system can be asked "who decided this?" Probing is done by the program, judgment by the model, execution by the program, sign-off by the human; yet no single role holds the whole picture.

The previous article asked why one name holds up. This one asks: once several names stand side by side, what is the relationship between them?

Last time I said Hermes's divine name is a shock absorber, set up to absorb the oscillations of hallucination in an uncontrollable probabilistic system. But a shock absorber has a fatal premise: it works only on a single black box. When the local Hermes and the cloud-side Muse must exchange structured commands through a pipe, the mythological shell shatters at once — programs believe no oracles; they recognize only status codes and trigger files. Two names together, the mythology is no longer a shock absorber but noise that hides the system's brittleness.

## II. The Main Line: A Tower Has Fallen

In 1978 the British management scholar Charles Handy published *Gods of Management: The Changing Work of Organizations*.

The book is most cited for a taxonomy: four organizational cultures, each mapped to a Greek deity — Zeus (power/club, spiderweb structure), Apollo (role/temple, colonnade structure), Athena (task/assault, grid structure), Dionysus (individual/existential, inverted constellation).

But that table is not the book's core. The core is a **narrative arc**, and the arc is the part most useful for today:

**Apollo built the bureaucratic pyramids of the industrial era.** From the end of World War I to the 1970s — assembly lines, big firms, functional specialization — role culture reached its peak. The faith of the period: put people in the right slots, define responsibilities, authority, and reporting lines clearly, and things will sort themselves out.

**Then the tower collapsed.** The oil crises of the 1970s, the rise of the knowledge economy; the rules changed faster than bureaucracy could update. The process architecture you spent two years building was already out of date at go-live. Hierarchy no longer guaranteed efficiency; it became the place where information was shaved layer by layer on its way up, and where each layer smuggled in its own private agenda.

**After the collapse, three deities stood up out of the rubble.** The Zeus type, hungry for power (gathering people around personal charisma and informal networks); the Athena type, where the competent take charge (ad hoc teams formed around a problem); the Dionysus type, who simply does not care about the organization ("I am the independent expert; come and court me").

Handy's real insight is not "organizations have four cultures" but that **the four are not parallel options but stand in a relation of succession.** Apollo is the mainstream; only after it collapses do the other three get a chance. And Apollo will look reliable again when knowledge complexity next rises — and collapse again.

**This cycle is now being replayed in AI.**

## III. The Monolithic Large Model Is That Tower

Apply this arc to today.

From 2020 to 2023 the AI industry held a belief almost no one voiced but everyone shared: build a big enough model and let it do everything. When GPT-3 appeared, the logic went: feed it all the text humanity has ever written and the model acquires understanding. This time is different; this time it is "compression."

It did compress. But past a certain scale the problems began to cluster:

**Cost.** Inference overhead for long contexts climbs steeply with token count; a model that reads two hundred thousand words, thinks, and calls tools costs real money on every invocation.

**Attention decay.** The fuller the context, the worse the recall for the middle. Material at the beginning or the end is still usable; in the middle it is largely lost. Not a bug — an inherent property of the Transformer architecture.

**Hallucination.** The inevitable product of probabilistic sampling. Ask it not to fabricate and it will try, but no guarantee is possible.

**Determinism.** Some tasks require input A to yield B, with zero error. A probabilistic system cannot deliver that.

The four together make the monolithic path's conclusion clear: **a single model cannot simultaneously meet the requirements of cost, interpretability, determinism, and expressive power.** The model is not too small; the architecture type is the wrong fit.

And that is precisely the definition of the Apollonian structure — one role, one process, carrying the entire complexity. When the complexity outgrows its load capacity, it does not degrade gracefully; it collapses.

**The monolithic large model is that tower. And it is collapsing.**

### How fast it is collapsing

Without exaggeration, the industry's response was faster than nearly any architectural pivot in the history of technology.

- From 2023, Function Calling became the mainstream: models no longer output raw text but output "I want to call this function with these parameters";
- On November 25, 2024, Anthropic open-sourced **MCP (Model Context Protocol)**, which it defined as "a new standard for connecting AI assistants to the systems where their data lives," covering three target categories: content repositories, business tools, and development environments.
- By 2026, multi-agent systems are no longer a research topic but the default architecture of every major vendor's flagship product.

**From "one big model solves everything" to "a swarm of specialist nodes, each handling a segment" — three years.** When Handy wrote this book, it took a full ten years to acknowledge the collapse of the Apollo tower.

## IV. How the Gods Divide Labor: An Honest Mapping Table

Now the mapping can be built. But one thing must be clear first:

**What follows is a structural isomorphism, not a historical correspondence.** Handy's four deities describe human organizational cultures of the 1970s; mapping them onto software architectures of the 2020s is an analogy, not a discovery. Analogies have limits — point out where I have overstepped.

| Handy Archetype | Organizational Form | Silicon Equivalent | Role in the System |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Apollo** | Role / temple culture <br> Colonnade structure, ruled by process and rules | **Deterministic code** <br> Schema validation, regex, permission checks, test assertions | The foundation that keeps the system from falling. **It is not smart; it is a load-bearing wall.** |
| **Zeus** | Power / club culture <br> Spiderweb structure, ruled by personal radiation | **Central orchestrator** <br> Intent decomposition, task dispatch, conflict arbitration | The only node with a global view. And the only single point of failure. |
| **Athena** | Task / assault culture <br> Grid structure, the competent take charge | **Dynamic expert cluster** <br> Assembled on demand, cross-domain parallelism, spun up temporarily | The assault unit for hard problems. Disbands when the task is done. |
| **Dionysus** | Individual / existential culture <br> Inverted constellation, the organization serves the individual | **Private instance / edge model** <br> Local data, local inference, not absorbed by the center | The counterweight against centralization. **The last line of defense for data sovereignty.** |

My system happens to have all four deities, and they are placed quite standardly:

- **Apollo** is the bash scripts and static assertions. They do not know what Hermes is; they only know "this message's format must conform to this schema." The least intelligent part, and the part you cannot afford to cut.
- **Zeus** is the cloud-side Muse. It has the global view — the only role that can read through all the material and render a judgment. Its point of highest value, and of greatest risk.
- **Athena** is the moment of invoking it — a problem appears, experts are roused into a temporary team, and they disband once it is solved.
- **Dionysus** is the local side. It runs locally, touches only local files, does not upload, and is not dispatched by the center — the only component in the whole system permitted to "disobey Zeus."

**Note the last one: the only component with the right to refuse the center is the runner script.**

This configuration is not a coincidence. It is the product of cost constraints — the center is expensive, local is cheap, so judgment goes to the center and execution stays local. An economic principle, not an architectural aesthetic.

## V. The Unix Razor: The Most Effective Antidote to "Divine Inflation"

Division of labor described; now a drug.

**In 1978, Doug McIlroy and colleagues at Bell Labs codified the Unix philosophy in the Unix Time-Sharing System special issue of the *Bell System Technical Journal*.** The four original tenets (the exact words, listed verbatim, because they are sharper than any paraphrase):

1. Make each program do one thing well.
2. To do a new job, build afresh rather than complicate old programs by adding new "features."
3. **Expect the output of every program to become the input of some other program.**
4. Use text as the universal interface.

The third and fourth are the key for this series. **In the Unix worldview a program is not a role; it is a segment of a pipe.** A program is not "supposed" to have an identity; its value consists entirely in whether its output can be consumed by the next program.

This is the exact opposite of Handy's framework. Handy asks "who calls the shots in the organization"; Unix asks "how things are put together."

**But in practice the two often amount to the same thing.** The essence of the MCP protocol is the network version of tenets three and four: each node exposes its capabilities as a standard interface, its output follows a unified format, and no one needs to know who sits at the far end.

### The two edges of the razor

The Unix philosophy carries one more tenet, later distilled into "Occam's razor": do not multiply entities beyond necessity. In Chinese-language discourse "Occam" is usually treated as an independent principle, but in an engineering context it is closer to a philosophical rendering of Unix tenet two — do not add features for imagined futures.

This principle does the most damage to AI architecture. **The industry's typical symptom today: everything must be made multi-agent.** A three-character string formatting pulls in a three-agent meeting; looking up one line in a log runs the full "plan–execute–reflect" cycle.

**Multi-agent comes at a price:** token consumption differs by orders of magnitude; communication overhead grows with the square of the node count; cross-node consistency is hard to guarantee; echo-chamber risk climbs with each round of discussion. Three agents debating a task is not three times more likely to produce good outcomes — it may be more likely, or less, depending on whether the discussion actually generated new information.

Unix would say: **first ask whether the problem needs multi-agent. In most cases it does not.** A dozen lines of `grep | awk` plus one assertion beats a round of debate among three agents — more reliable, cheaper, faster.

**Occam's razor here is not an aesthetic preference; it is a cost discipline.** Every "shall we bring in an agent?" decision should default to "no" and flip to "yes" only when argued into it.

## VI. The Inversion: The Smarter This System, the Longer Its Shadow

By now a healthy technical narrative is complete: the monolith has fallen → the federation has been built → the disciplines have been defined.

But this narrative has a fatal premise: **that the system can know what it is doing.**

Current systems cannot.

This section is the turning point of the whole piece — and the one most worth reading slowly.

### Gödel's incompleteness: the ceiling of the foundation

**In 1931, Kurt Gödel** proved a theorem that holds for every formal system:

Any formal system powerful enough to express its own basic arithmetic, if it is consistent (free of contradiction), **cannot prove its own consistency from within the system**. There must exist at least one true proposition that is unprovable inside it.

This is not merely a mathematical result. Its reach extends to the foundation of the whole logical edifice.

**Translated to AI systems:** in a multi-agent system, if one Agent's job is to "check whether other Agents' outputs are correct," that checker is itself inside the system. **It cannot completely check itself.** Introduce a meta-checker to check it, and the meta-checker is likewise inside the system, likewise unable to self-certify. The chain must terminate somewhere — and the termination point is an unproven assumption.

**Any self-referential system, at any scale, ultimately rests on a foundation it cannot verify.**

Not a bug. Mathematics.

### What self-reference looks like in engineering: the strange loop

**Douglas Hofstadter** made this more concrete in *Gödel, Escher, Bach* (*GEB*, 1979), with the concept of the "strange loop": a system jumps out from some level, looks back at the level it left, then incorporates that very act of jumping into the system's own definition.

His example is the mind: the synapses of a neuron do not know what they are thinking, but a network of enough synapses "knows." That knowing does not need to be recorded at the neuron level.

**In AI systems the strange loop has an engineering form: the introspection loop.**

The current standard is the Reflexion architecture: the executor produces a result, the critic reads it and points out problems, the executor reads the criticism and revises, and generates again. Repeated until convergence.

This architecture is useful — usefully, in fact, to a striking degree. **But it is simultaneously a perfect self-referential trap.**

**Problem one: the checker and the checked share the same foundation.** The critic Agent and the executor Agent run on the same pre-trained weights, the same training data, the same architecture. The executor's bias is most likely invisible to the critic — because that bias is precisely the shape of the distribution. The critic is not an external reviewer; it is the same person wearing a pair of glasses.

> This architecture is not my invention; it has an original paper: Shinn, N., Cassano, F., Berman, E., Gopinath, A., Narasimhan, K., & Yao, S. "Reflexion: Language Agents with Verbal Reinforcement Learning." arXiv:2303.11366, 2023.

**Problem two: what many rounds of introspection converge on may not be truth but consensus.** Three Agents debating for ten rounds may reach an "agreement" that is merely a shared hallucination. **The strength of consensus and correctness have no causal link**; more rounds only make everyone more confident in the conclusion.

**Problem three: the risk grows in proportion to recursive depth.** More reflection layers, and token cost rises exponentially while error accumulation is amplified in lockstep. And if a reflection layer itself errs, that error is treated by every subsequent layer as a "corrected fact."

**Hofstadter would say: the strange loop is at once the origin of mind and its boundary.** It cannot be abolished — without self-reference there is no self-knowledge. But it cannot be trusted either — **that it knows it knows is not that it knows.**

### Pits I stepped into myself

I do not want this section to be only theory.

I set a rule for my own system: after modifying a live system, acceptance cannot rest on "re-reading the file I just wrote"; it must require runtime evidence after a restart. The rule was taught to me by being burned in actual work — it was not derived from any theory.

**The essence of this rule is an application of Gödel's theorem.** I modify a script, then read it back to confirm it looks right. That act has no value whatsoever: the person who wrote it and the person reading it are the same source of information. Not verification — a self-loop.

**This is why the cross-restart is needed.** It forcibly introduces a fact from outside the system — the process actually started, the file was actually generated, the assertion actually fired. It is the only mechanism that can break the self-reference.

**In AI systems we do not have the luxury of a cross-restart.** A model modifies its own prompt and then evaluates its own output — evaluator and evaluated share the same context, the same set of weights, every assumption of the same inference pass. The most perfectly closed strange loop in history.

**So: for every claim of "AI self-improvement," ask one question — where is the external anchor?**

## VII. Loss of Control: This Is Not an Accident, It Is the Price

This section will be kept short, but it cannot be cut.

**Kevin Kelly** offered an uncomfortable judgment in *Out of Control* (1994): **the emergence of intelligence must be paid for with the surrender of control.**

A bee swarm solves problems no human designer could design, because each bee follows only simple local rules and no single bee grasps the whole — **holistic intelligence comes from the absence of a center.** The converse holds: demand complete control, predictable outputs, zero surprises, and you limit the solution space the system can discover.

**In multi-agent systems this is the same trade-off in its engineering form:**

| Pursuit | Cost |
|---|---|
| Central orchestration (Zeus) | Single point of failure, context drift, cascading collapse |
| Distributed autonomy (Athena cluster) | Communication overhead, cross-node inconsistency, consensus hallucination |
| Strict rules (Apollo guardrails) | Rigidity, inability to handle the long tail, the rules themselves require maintenance |

**There is no free cell in this table. Every cell carries a cost, and every cell must be chosen explicitly rather than pretended not to exist.**

The 4C framework that Ohmae Kenichi set out in *The Borderless World* (1990) — capital, companies, consumers, communications — is being pushed even further in this direction: compute, models, invocations, and data are becoming a currency and a labor force that flow across borders; the boundaries now live at the protocol layer, not the physical one.

But Ohmae was describing the penetration of capital and goods; what concerns me more is that **this network has no citizens.** Multi-agent clusters flow freely at the protocol layer, yet carry no identity, no liability, no boundaries. An extraordinarily efficient collaboration mechanism — and a system with no accountability mechanism.

**This is also why the last gate in my system is a human signature** — not because I trust the model, but precisely because I do not trust any system to set its own boundaries for itself.

## VIII. Conclusion: The Federation of Gods Has No Priest

**In the story Handy tells, the gods are plural and the organization is their arena.** Each deity has its own jurisdiction, its own followers, its own logic of conduct.

**A multi-agent system has copied this structure wholesale.** But there is one critical difference.

In Handy's mythology there is no engineer. Organizations grow naturally, power waxes and wanes, and no one has designed who obeys whom.

**A multi-agent system, by contrast, is designed.** Each Agent's duties, boundaries, permissions, and communication protocols are written down by a human. Every row in those four mapping tables corresponds to a line of code.

**This means: the federation of gods is not the product of belief; it is the product of engineering choice.** It can be redesigned, adjusted, or have a particular node switched off.

**This is at once its strength and its limit.** We can choose who listens to whom — my system gives the final veto to the runner script. Counter-intuitive, but correct: it is the component with the lowest cost and the smallest price of a misjudgment.

**Handy's tower collapsing is history.**
**Whether your tower will collapse, when, and which new structure to use after it has — that is an engineering question.**
**Do not hand it over to mythology.**

## Note 1: The Federation Is a Tactic of a Finite Game

This article's conclusion stops at "it is an engineering choice." **But looking back from Part 5, one will find that this engineering choice has a more precise name.**

James P. Carse distinguished in *Finite and Infinite Games* (Free Press, 1986): a finite game is played in order to win, an infinite game in order to keep playing; **the rules of the two serve opposite functions — a finite game's rules guarantee that it ends, an infinite game's rules guarantee that it does not.**

Under this framework, **splitting a monolithic model into a multi-agent federation is a paradigmatic finite-game move.**

The reasoning is direct: a monolithic model has a theoretical upper limit on its capabilities, and that limit cannot be enumerated in advance — it has the shape of an infinite game. The federation's whole point is **to draw a manageable boundary around an object that has no terminus**: to break a problem that cannot be solved in one piece into a set of sub-tasks, each independently verifiable. **This does not make the infinite game more fun; it installs a terminus on it.**

So the conclusion of section three needs one addition: **the Gödel loop is not a defect of the federation; it is the proof that the federation-as-device works.** The federation requires a structure in which "each god sees only its own patch of sky" precisely because **a complete view is unavailable from inside the system.** And the moment you try to stitch all the gods' patches together to complete the whole, you arrive at the thing Part 5 describes — **a rule that you cannot write yourself.**

**And under this framework the naming "the gods" shifts position as well.** In Part 1 I said a divine name is a shock absorber and a boundary-marker; in this article I said the federation is an engineering choice. Part 5 joins the two: **to call a division-of-labor structure designed to win by the name of a god — that single act of naming conceals its double identity at once (an effective engineering device / a wager that has already been settled).**

The four Unix principles make sense here as well. Earlier I treated them as the sharpness of doing less; **but their more precise character is this: a set of rules that lets a system run on indefinitely without anyone having to announce its completion.** The filter keeps what is wanted and discards what is not; on failure, exit silently; small tools chained into big tools — **none of these produces a signal of "done."**

**And this is what I did not see when I wrote this article: Unix philosophy is in fact on the infinite-game side of the line.** I used its sharpness to argue for a finite-game federation; **there is a fault line between them that I did not cross over.** It looks so well-suited to multi-agent systems precisely because it does not care about winning or losing at all — what it cares about is whether this thing can still go on tomorrow.
